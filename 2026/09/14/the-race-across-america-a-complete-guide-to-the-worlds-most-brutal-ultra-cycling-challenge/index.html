The Race Across America: A Complete Guide to the World’s Most Brutal Ultra-Cycling Challenge

By Justus
September 14, 2026 15 Min Read
0

The Race Across America, commonly referred to as RAAM, stands as one of the most grueling ultra-endurance cycling competitions on the planet.

Unlike grand tours such as the Tour de France, Giro d’Italia, or Vuelta a España, RAAM is not divided into daily stages. Instead, it functions as a single, nonstop race against the clock spanning the entire United States.

The classic course stretches from Oceanside, California, on the Pacific shore to Atlantic City, New Jersey, on the Atlantic coast, totaling roughly 3,000 miles (4,800–5,000 km) depending on the specific year’s routing.

Competitors must traverse the continent within a firm overall deadline while confronting mountain passes, scorching deserts, violent storms, heavy traffic, exhaustion, and severe sleep deprivation.

What sets this event apart is that the timing never pauses.

A cyclist may halt to eat, rest, fix a mechanical issue, or change clothes, but the race clock keeps ticking.

This fundamental difference distinguishes RAAM from traditional professional road racing.

Success is not merely about raw speed.

It demands juggling pace, fueling, rest, equipment, weather, navigation, crew coordination, and psychological toughness all at once for over a week.

The competition has run since 1982 and has earned global recognition as one of the ultimate tests in ultra-distance cycling.

The Origins and History of RAAM

1982: The Great American Bike Race

RAAM traces its beginnings to 1982, when it was first called the Great American Bike Race.

Cyclist John Marino organized the event with the goal of establishing an ultra-distance challenge spanning the United States.

Only four competitors took part in the inaugural edition:

The original course ran from Santa Monica, California, to the Empire State Building in New York City.

Lon Haldeman claimed victory in that first race, finishing in 9 days, 20 hours, and 2 minutes.

The following year, the event was renamed the Race Across America.

What started with just four participants eventually grew into an international ultra-cycling contest drawing athletes from many nations.

How the Race Has Evolved

The route has shifted significantly over the decades.

Early editions featured various western start points and eastern finishes, including endpoints in New York, Atlantic City, Savannah, and Washington, D.C.

For many years, the modern event was associated with the Oceanside-to-Annapolis course before the finish line was moved to Atlantic City.

Despite these changes, the core concept has remained remarkably stable:

Cycle across America as fast as possible, with no stages or rest days built into the race.

The RAAM Route

The contemporary Race Across America course starts on the Pacific coast at Oceanside, California, and heads eastward toward Atlantic City, New Jersey.

The precise distance fluctuates due to annual route modifications, road closures, and logistical factors.

The 2026 route measured approximately 4,938 km, or about 3,069 miles.

Climbing Demands

One of the most daunting features of RAAM is the sheer volume of elevation gain.

Historically, the course has included roughly 170,000 feet of climbing, though the exact figure depends on the route.

That translates to more than 51,000 meters of ascent.

To put it in perspective, competitors are essentially pairing a transcontinental distance with the equivalent of scaling some of Europe’s major mountain passes over and over.

Geographic Challenges

A RAAM cyclist encounters wildly different environments during the journey.

The environmental shift is dramatic.

A rider might start in warm Pacific sunshine, cross blistering desert roads, climb above 3,000 meters in Colorado, and later face humidity, thunderstorms, and cooler eastern conditions.

In 2026, participants dealt with desert temperatures surpassing 43°C and high-altitude terrain exceeding 3,300 meters.

Understanding the RAAM Format

The most crucial thing to grasp about RAAM is that it is not a stage race.

There are no:

The race runs continuously from start to finish.

The clock starts when the rider begins and does not stop until the finish line.

Riders Decide When to Sleep

This creates one of RAAM’s signature strategic dilemmas.

A solo competitor can pause to sleep if needed, but every minute spent resting is a minute rivals might gain.

As a result, front-runners historically sleep very little.

Official RAAM documentation identifies sleep management as one of the essential elements of successful racing.

The outcome is a delicate balancing act.

Ride too slowly and you miss cutoff times.

Ride too hard and you risk burning out.

Sleep too much and you lose precious time.

Sleep too little and you may become unsafe or unable to maintain a useful pace.

How Long Does RAAM Take?

The quickest solo riders can finish RAAM in well under nine days, while many successful competitors take roughly nine to twelve days.

The race’s official time limit matters more than any average finishing time.

For the traditional men’s solo category, the standard allowance has been 288 hours — 12 days.

The exact allowance should always be verified against the rules for the specific edition.

What Happens If a Rider Misses the Time Limit?

A cyclist cannot simply arrive whenever they please and still earn an official RAAM finish.

The event includes intermediate checkpoints and cutoffs.

These exist partly for safety and partly to ensure riders stay on a realistic path toward the finish.

Historical RAAM rules identify intermediate checkpoints where riders must arrive within prescribed windows, with the final cutoff serving as a hard deadline.

This adds another layer of psychological pressure.

A rider may be hundreds of miles into the event but still need to calculate whether they are sufficiently ahead of the clock.

What Are RAAM Time Stations?

Time Stations are designated points along the route where competitors must check in.

They allow race headquarters to monitor progress and maintain an official record of each rider’s position and elapsed time.

Historically, RAAM has used more than 50 Time Stations spread across the course.

They are generally spaced at intervals of roughly 50–100 miles, though the exact arrangement can vary.

Official format documentation describes Time Stations as a fundamental component of the race’s tracking system.

Why Are Time Stations Important?

They serve several functions.

Tracking: Race officials know where riders are.

Timing: Progress against cutoffs can be assessed.

Safety: Officials can monitor riders who are struggling.

Penalties: Time penalties may be recorded and served at designated locations.

Navigation: They form a structured sequence through an otherwise enormous route.

For riders, however, they also serve as psychological milestones.

Instead of thinking about the entire 3,000-mile journey, a rider can focus on reaching the next Time Station.

How Does Solo RAAM Racing Work?

Solo RAAM is perhaps the purest expression of the event.

One rider is responsible for covering the entire distance.

There is no teammate who can take over.

There is no peloton to shelter from the wind.

There is no domestique to collect bottles.

There is no team leader surrounded by nine professional teammates.

The rider is physically alone on the bicycle, though supported by a crew traveling alongside.

This makes solo RAAM fundamentally different from conventional road racing.

Can RAAM Riders Draft?

No.

Drafting is prohibited.

That is an important distinction because professional road racing is heavily influenced by aerodynamic shelter from other riders.

RAAM riders are essentially racing individually against the clock.

They therefore face the full aerodynamic cost of riding into the wind.

Official RAAM rules have historically prohibited drafting and require racers to maintain separation from other competitors.

How Does RAAM Team Racing Work?

RAAM is not restricted to solo competitors.

Teams can enter the event using relay-style strategies.

The riders share the distance.

One rider may be on the road while the others travel in support vehicles, eat, recover, or sleep.

Teams can therefore maintain much higher continuous cycling speeds than a solo rider.

Official RAAM format documentation explains that teams can rotate riders in shifts that may last from minutes to several hours, depending on strategy and terrain.

Why Teams Are So Much Faster

Consider the basic mathematics.

A solo rider might need to average roughly 250 miles per day to finish within 12 days.

A well-organized eight-person team can divide the physical workload among eight athletes.

This means riders can produce much higher power when they are on the bicycle and recover while teammates continue moving.

It transforms the event from an individual endurance challenge into a complex exercise in team management and logistics.

The Importance of the RAAM Support Crew

A successful RAAM attempt is rarely just about the cyclist.

For solo competitors, the support crew can be the difference between finishing and abandoning.

The Crew Chief

The crew chief is particularly important.

They effectively become the strategic manager of the attempt.

During a difficult period, the rider may be exhausted, dehydrated, or mentally overwhelmed.

The crew chief must still make rational decisions.

Should the rider stop?

Should they eat?

Should they sleep?

Can they maintain the required pace?

Is the rider becoming unsafe?

These decisions can determine the outcome of the race.

How Much Sleep Do RAAM Riders Get?

Very little compared with normal life.

This is one of the most extreme elements of the event.

Historically, some front-running solo competitors have slept for only around 90 minutes per day during parts of the race.

A rider attempting simply to finish within the time limit still has to keep sleep under tight control.

It is to determine the minimum amount of sleep that allows the rider to continue safely and effectively.

The Dangers of Race Across America

RAAM is an exceptionally demanding event.

The principal risks include:

Sleep Deprivation

Perhaps the biggest danger is prolonged lack of sleep.

Unlike a conventional race, RAAM takes place around the clock.

Extreme Heat

The route crosses some of the hottest parts of the United States.

Desert temperatures can become extreme during the daytime.

Cold and Altitude

Colorado introduces a completely different set of problems.

Riders can experience cold temperatures and high-altitude climbing after spending hours or days in low-altitude environments.

Traffic

RAAM takes place predominantly on open roads.

Riders therefore have to deal with normal road traffic.

Weather

Mechanical Problems

A broken drivetrain, wheel, or other critical component can cost valuable time.

The support crew must be capable of repairing or replacing equipment quickly.

How Do Riders Qualify for RAAM?

RAAM is not generally an event where an inexperienced cyclist can simply pay an entry fee and turn up.

Solo entrants have historically been required to demonstrate their ability through qualifying performances or other recognized criteria.

The rules have allowed riders who believe they are sufficiently experienced to petition the Race Director, providing an athletic résumé and explanation of their qualifications.

Team qualification requirements have historically been less restrictive.

Nevertheless, completing RAAM requires a level of endurance far beyond that required for an ordinary sportive or century ride.

RAAM has produced a remarkable collection of ultra-endurance cycling legends.

Lon Haldeman

Lon Haldeman won the first RAAM in 1982 and repeated his victory in 1983.

His first crossing took 9 days, 20 hours, and 2 minutes.

Haldeman is one of the foundational figures in American ultra-distance cycling.

Pete Penseyres

Pete Penseyres won RAAM in 1984 and 1986.

His 1986 victory was particularly fast, taking just 8 days, 9 hours, and 47 minutes.

The Famous RAAM Winners

The historical winners list records Strasser as the six-time winner and Robič as a five-time champion. The most recent editions added Jimmy Rönn, Philipp Kaider, Leah Goldstein, Allan Jefferson, and Isa Pulver to RAAM’s list of solo champions.

The 2021 RAAM Victory of Leah Goldstein

One of the most significant recent RAAM victories came in 2021, when Canada’s Leah Goldstein won the solo race.

Her victory was historic.

Goldstein had previously worked as a police officer and had competed at a high level in cycling and other sports.

She became the first woman to win the overall solo Race Across America outright, rather than merely winning the women’s category.

Her achievement demonstrated the level of endurance required to compete at the sharp end of RAAM regardless of gender.

Philipp Kaider: The Latest RAAM Champion

The most recent completed edition, RAAM 2026, was won by Austrian Philipp Kaider.

Kaider successfully defended the title he won in 2025.

He crossed the approximately 4,938-kilometer course from Oceanside to Atlantic City in 8 days, 18 hours, and 58 minutes.

His 2026 victory was particularly impressive because of the conditions.

RAAM Records

RAAM records are difficult to compare directly because the route has changed repeatedly.

Nevertheless, some performances have become legendary.

Christoph Strasser’s 2014 victory was one of the fastest in RAAM history.

The event’s modern record-setting performances demonstrate how dramatically ultra-cycling has evolved since the inaugural 1982 race.

The combination of better bicycles, improved aerodynamics, power meters, nutrition, navigation technology, training methods, and highly sophisticated support crews has transformed performance.

RAAM Compared with the Tour de France

Although both events cover thousands of miles, they are fundamentally different.

RAAM has sometimes been described as the equivalent of completing a Tour de France distance in roughly half the time.

But that comparison can be misleading.

The Tour de France is a sophisticated team sport featuring stages, tactics, sprint trains, breakaways, and mountain-top finishes.

RAAM is closer to a transcontinental individual time trial combined with an extreme endurance expedition.

RAAM Versus Other Ultra-Cycling Races

RAAM is not the only major ultra-distance cycling event.

The biggest distinction is the support structure.

Many ultra-endurance bikepacking races are self-supported.

RAAM is different.

The event is built around support crews and support vehicles.

That means RAAM is as much a logistical competition as it is a cycling competition.

Race Across the West

RAAM also has a shorter counterpart known as the Race Across the West, or RAW.

RAW uses the western portion of the RAAM course and allows riders to tackle a major transcontinental-style ultra-distance challenge without crossing the entire United States.

The 2026 course information shows RAW following the RAAM route from the start through to the Durango Time Station before diverging from the full RAAM challenge.

For riders who are not yet ready for the full Race Across America, RAW can provide a useful stepping stone.

Is RAAM the World’s Toughest Bicycle Race?

RAAM is frequently described as “the world’s toughest bicycle race.”

That description is subjective, but there is a strong argument for it.

The combination of distance, elevation, weather, traffic, sleep deprivation, and continuous racing makes RAAM exceptionally demanding.

It is also important to remember that the race is not simply about physical fitness.

RAAM therefore tests the entire endurance system surrounding a cyclist.

How Many People Finish RAAM?

The completion rate varies considerably by year and category.

Historically, a substantial proportion of solo entrants have failed to finish.

Some reports have put non-finish rates at around half of the field in particularly difficult editions.

That is why simply becoming an official RAAM finisher is itself considered a major achievement.

Winning is an entirely different level.

What Makes RAAM So Difficult?

There is no single answer.

It is the combination of everything.

The Distance

Three thousand miles is an enormous distance.

The Clock

There is no opportunity to wait for tomorrow.

The Mountains

The route contains tens of thousands of meters of climbing.

The Weather

Riders experience multiple climate zones.

Sleep Deprivation

The rider must make difficult decisions while increasingly fatigued.

The Mental Challenge

The finish line may be more than a week away.

The Logistics

Everything from food to tires must be managed.

The Road

Unlike a closed professional circuit, much of the route uses public roads.

It is the interaction of these factors that makes RAAM so formidable.

What Do RAAM Riders Eat?

Nutrition is another crucial component.

A rider may burn thousands of calories each day.

Food therefore has to be readily available while the bicycle is moving.

Can an Amateur Enter RAAM?

Yes, RAAM is not exclusively for professional cyclists.

That is one of the fascinating things about the event.

Participants have come from a wide variety of backgrounds.

Some are experienced ultra-endurance athletes.

Others are ordinary cyclists who have spent years preparing for the challenge.

However, “amateur” should not be confused with “unprepared.”

A successful RAAM entrant needs enormous endurance, careful preparation, a capable crew, and experience with very long-distance riding.

Solo entrants must also satisfy the applicable qualification requirements.

How Do You Train for RAAM?

Training for RAAM is very different from preparing for a conventional cycling event.

A rider needs to develop several abilities simultaneously.

Build Enormous Endurance

Long-distance riding must become normal rather than exceptional.

Practise Consecutive Long Days

A 300-mile ride followed by another 300-mile ride is very different from a single ultra-distance effort.

Practise Riding at Night

Night riding is unavoidable.

Develop a Nutrition Strategy

Eating and drinking must become part of the riding routine.

Test Equipment

A small problem that is manageable during a 100-mile ride can become disastrous over 3,000 miles.

Train the Crew

The support team needs practice too.

Practise Sleep Management

Riders need to understand how their bodies respond to reduced sleep.

Learn to Ride in Difficult Conditions

Heat, wind, rain, and darkness are all part of the challenge.

The Role of Mental Strength

Perhaps the most underestimated aspect of RAAM is psychology.

Imagine completing 1,500 miles and knowing you still have roughly another 1,500 to go.

Then imagine doing that while exhausted and having slept only a few hours.

The rider must repeatedly convince themselves to keep moving.

This is why experienced RAAM riders often break the race into smaller objectives.

Rather than thinking:

“I have another 1,500 miles.”

they think:

“I need to reach the next Time Station.”

Then the next one.

And the next.

Eventually, the Atlantic Ocean appears.

RAAM and Charity

RAAM has also developed an important connection with charitable fundraising.

Many riders use their participation to raise money or awareness for causes ranging from veterans’ welfare to medical research and humanitarian organizations.

This adds another dimension to the challenge.

A rider is not necessarily crossing America simply to win.

They may also be carrying a cause, fundraising campaign, or personal mission.

The Future of Race Across America

The future of RAAM entered an important new phase in 2026.

Following the completion of the 2026 event, the organizers announced that RAAM, Race Across the West, and Race Across the East would be paused for the 2027 calendar year.

The organizers said the pause would allow them to review the races, their formats, and the overall participant experience before determining the future direction of the events.

This is significant because RAAM has more than four decades of history.

The organizers have indicated that their intention is to bring the event back in an improved form, but the timing and exact format of any return have not been established.

For now, the 2026 edition represents the most recent completed RAAM.

The Legacy of Race Across America

Few cycling events can claim a history quite like RAAM.

From four riders crossing America in 1982 to international fields containing elite ultra-endurance athletes, the event has evolved enormously.

Its greatest champions — including Lon Haldeman, Pete Penseyres, Rob Kish, Wolfgang Fasching, Jure Robič, and Christoph Strasser — have established an extraordinary record of endurance.

More recently, riders such as Leah Goldstein, Isa Pulver, and Philipp Kaider have demonstrated that RAAM continues to attract competitors capable of pushing the limits of human endurance.

The 2026 victory of Philipp Kaider added another chapter to that history.

Race Across America: The Ultimate Endurance Cycling Challenge

The Race Across America is difficult to compare with any conventional cycling race.

It is too long to be a normal road race.

It is too fast to be simply a bike tour.

It is too strategically complex to be reduced to a test of fitness.

And it is too physically demanding to be treated as an ordinary sportive.

RAAM is an extraordinary combination of cycling, endurance, logistics, strategy, and survival.

A rider must cross approximately 3,000 miles, climb tens of thousands of meters, deal with multiple climate zones, and manage sleep while racing against a relentless clock.

For solo competitors, there is nowhere to hide.

There is no teammate to take a turn.

No peloton to provide shelter.

No rest day.

No overnight neutralization.

Just the bicycle, the road, the support crew, and the clock.

That is why, more than four decades after its creation, Race Across America remains one of the ultimate challenges in endurance cycling.
